Ringo

A pet robot that is its own wheels.

Concept

The body is the wheels. Two big frosted halo rings roll on the floor. A sealed pod with the face hangs inside them like a pendulum. Its battery and a steel keel sit low, so Ringo can't tip over and never has to balance. Each ring has a gear molded on its inside, and one tiny N20 motor per ring drives it. There's no axle, hub, bearing, suspension or balance loop.

Why it wins

  • Iconic at a glance. A glowing face floating in two hoops. It doesn't look like any car, puck, dog or droid, so a 2-second clip reads as Ringo.
  • Can't be knocked over. The pendulum always rights it, the rings are the bumper, and a 160 mm wheel rolls over rug edges, cables and door sills that stop small-wheeled pets.
  • Personality for free. The pod nods when it starts, leans into turns and wobbles when it stops. That's physics, not animation code.
  • Cheapest drivetrain possible. Two $3.50 motors and two molded rings (one tool, flipped). About $36 to build, so it can sell for $129.
  • Useful every day. Home check-in camera, pet-watch, a talking timer and reminders that roll over to you, a night-light, and a doorbell or alarm that comes to find you.

Production

Part count: 6 molding tools for the robot and 1 for the dock. That makes 11 molded pieces, 1 PCB, 4 screws and 4 pins. Left and right rings and treads come from the same tool, flipped.

DFM

  • The pod splits at x=0, and both halves pull straight with no side actions. Walls are 2.0 mm with 4 screw bosses molded in.
  • The ring is a cored U-channel with 2.5 mm walls, open on one face so the core pulls straight. It has 24 ribs and a module-1 internal gear molded in. The TPU tread is 2-shot overmolded into a locking groove.
  • Rollers and pinions come from one POM family tool. The eye lens is PMMA with a hard coat.
  • Prototype: every part prints on a 0.4 mm nozzle. Use PETG for the shells and translucent PETG for the rings, and print the treads as TPU bands. ASCII STL and STEP files are in /cad.

Assembly (about 8 min)

  1. Snap the main board, battery and keel into the rear shell. Seat the motors in their saddles and press the pinions onto the shafts.
  2. Fit the display and lens into the front shell, then plug the display and camera flex cables into the board.
  3. Close the shells with 4 screws. The belly pads sit flush.
  4. Press each motor inward on its spring saddle, slide a ring over the hub, then release so the pinion meshes. Press in 2 idler pins per side.
  5. Park it on the dock, and the end-of-line test runs over the pogo USB.
